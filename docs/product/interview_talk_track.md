# Interview talk track

## The 60-second version

My problem statement is that modern education is broken. Most of the things that we are taught in school or college has no usefulness/relevance to the job that we do or the life that we live. I studied physics, chemistry, mathematics and biology in school and chemistry in my undergraduate, but my current profession is product manager with absolutely no relevance. So many years of wasted education and by extension productivity—and I can say for sure that this is true for millions of people across the world.

Education is an aspiration for a better life which then should be oriented towards earth's welfare. School education starts around age 5–6 and a four-year college ends around 22. We have a sixteen-year period for education. What we need is to start orienting the child's education around a tangible real-world profession and related outcomes based on the aspirations of the family and child, while still preserving common foundations and the ability to change direction.

UPSC is a useful example because clearing the exam is a real hard criterion. But the aspiration should not stop at “become an IAS officer”: the learner should uncover what type of public service they actually want to pursue, what people or problems they want to serve, and what viable alternatives preserve that purpose if the exam is not cleared.

## The product question

How might a learner and family move from an exam label or broad aspiration to a specific understanding of the contribution they want to make, and then orient education towards the knowledge, practice, experience, examination, and alternatives required to pursue it?

## What is intentionally not yet claimed

There is no chosen product prototype yet. The next prototype should test one human decision—such as clarifying an aspiration, comparing adjacent pathways, or deciding whether a family is ready to commit to a pathway—and produce evidence about that decision. The earlier chemistry application is archived prior work, not proof of this thesis.

## What would change next

- replace a single GATE course profile with a pathway profile;
- add aspiration discovery and family context;
- add capability and experience maps alongside academic lessons;
- add pathway gates such as UPSC, NEET, license, apprenticeship, or employer assessment;
- generate a learner evidence record and an institution deployment playbook;
- evaluate transition outcomes, not only lesson completion.

## GTM and diffusion argument

I would not begin by asking the poorest families to bear the risk of an unproven education model. I would first learn with families who have the agency and resources to experiment, especially urban technology-worker parents already questioning conventional schooling. The point is not to build an elite product. The point is to generate credible evidence, reduce uncertainty, and turn the first deployment into a reusable pathway for wider institutional adoption.

A class-6-to-career pathway takes 12–15 years to observe fully. The first deployment must therefore measure leading indicators: mastery, reasoning, writing, applied problem solving, service orientation, wellbeing, and progression against the relevant hard gate. As those indicators are validated against later outcomes, subsequent deployments can evaluate implementation quality much earlier instead of waiting for the whole life cycle to finish.

There is also a capital-formation logic. If early adopter families see meaningful outcomes, they can become advocates, mentors, connectors to employers and institutions, and potential sources of grants, donations, or philanthropic capital. The aim is not merely to use affluent families as proof points for poorer families. It is to create a constituency that has experienced the model and is willing to help finance and diffuse it as public-interest infrastructure.

That requires showing funders more than inspiring stories: outcome measurement, leading-indicator validity, cost per learner, additionality, learner wellbeing, and a transparent mechanism for reaching families with fewer resources.

## Viksit Bharat and EkStep's long horizon

I see this as aligned with Viksit Bharat @2047: a multi-decade development vision needs an education system that compounds capability across generations, not one that optimizes only the next exam or placement cycle. A class-6 learner today may contribute to India's institutions and economy in the 2030s and 2040s.

That is also where I connect with EkStep's seed-to-forest philosophy. The first deployment should prioritize validity, learner agency, safety, and reusable infrastructure over quick but shallow growth. Doing it right is what makes doing it faster possible for the next institution. Each deployment should leave behind better content mappings, assessments, governance rules, leading indicators, and implementation knowledge.

The sequence is:

`careful first pathway → trustworthy evidence → reusable public-good toolkit → shorter and safer next deployment`

## Seed-to-forest deployment story

I would describe the scale path as a sequence of increasingly demanding deployments: start with urban families who can experiment; learn what works; move into a few gram panchayats with local teachers and families; turn that into a district playbook; replicate across neighboring districts; then make the case to a state government; and only after state-level evidence, approach national platforms and government.

Each stage has a different implementation problem. The gram panchayat stage tests language, offline access, teacher capacity, and local ownership. The district stage tests staffing, administration, funding, and measurement. The state stage tests curriculum alignment, examination pathways, procurement, and public financing. The national stage tests interoperability and whether the building blocks can become a shared public good.

The first pilot is therefore a seed, not a miniature version of the final national system. It must leave behind evidence, relationships, and a deployment playbook that the next context can adapt.

## More precise aspiration, more resilient outcomes

The self-discovery layer should not stop at “become an engineer” or “join public service.” It should help the learner identify the type of engineering or public service they want to pursue and the people or problems they want to serve. That gives the curriculum a meaningful organizing purpose.

Because UPSC, NEET, and IIT-JEE are selective gates, the pathway must also maintain alternatives. A learner who wants mechanical engineering should compare that branch across institutions rather than automatically accept an unrelated branch for prestige. A public-service learner should have adjacent routes into state services, health administration, education, rural development, research, or other forms of civic contribution.

The objective is not to maximize exam selection at any cost. It is to develop capable people oriented toward *loka kshema*—the welfare of people and the earth—and help them find the most credible route to contribute.

## Questions I would ask in the interview

- Which pathway and population should EkStep use as the first bounded deployment?
- What would count as a credible leading indicator, and who should validate it?
- How do we prevent early adopters' privilege from being mistaken for product impact?
- Which building blocks should be reusable across pathways and contexts?
- What evidence would allow a later government or low-income deployment to adopt with less risk?

## The honest limitation

The thesis is not evidence that aspiration-led education improves outcomes yet. The next step is to choose a narrowly scoped prototype with explicit safeguards, comparison measures, and learner, parent, and educator feedback.
